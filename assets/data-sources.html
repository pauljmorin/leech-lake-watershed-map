Data sources

Leech Lake Watershed

Every dataset this map draws on, what it is, where it comes from, and how it was turned into what the map shows. Each section states the interpretation method — the exact rules used to turn the raw data into the published layer — so a reader can reproduce every classification from the references plus the stated rules. All data comes from public sources; the map holds no data of its own. Aerial imagery is streamed from the providers’ services and is never downloaded onto this site’s server.

A note on freshness. Most of these layers are snapshots, pulled on the dates given — November 20, 2026, unless a section says otherwise. A layer is a record of its pull date, not a live feed. Where a source updates on a schedule, the schedule is noted. The exceptions are the satellite layers (next section): they stream live from their providers at view time and are never snapshots.

Satellite imagery

Sentinel-2 — latest imagery

Live satellite imagery of the watershed from the Sentinel-2 mission: 10 m multispectral scenes in natural colour, streamed from Esri’s Sentinel-2 image service at view time. Nothing is downloaded or hosted on this site — the viewer points at the service the same way it streams the aerial photography and WMS layers, and the imagery the reader sees is whatever the service’s mosaic holds at that moment. The layer is a prototype and is OFF by default.

Interpretation method. Each map tile is an exportImage request against the service in Web Mercator (EPSG:3857), rendered with the service’s “Natural Color with DRA” raster function (bands 4, 3, 2 — red, green, blue — with dynamic range adjustment). No mosaic rule and no time filter are sent, so the service’s own mosaic default decides which scene appears at each place: its most recent acquisition, with no cloud filtering added by this viewer beyond the service’s own default. The service is time-enabled and updated daily, holding the past 14 months of imagery. While the layer is switched on, the panel shows the acquisition date of the newest scene over the current view, read live from the service’s catalog (its acquisitiondate field); the mosaic may blend neighbouring scenes, so the date can vary across the view. Caveat: “latest” means the newest scene the service holds, not a clear one — the latest scene may be cloudy, and in a cloudy stretch it may be weeks old. In winter it will mostly show snow and ice.

Source: Esri, Sentinel-2 image service (Sentinel-2 on AWS via Esri; imagery © European Commission, European Space Agency) — sentinel.arcgis.com/arcgis/rest/services/Sentinel2/ImageServer. 10 m resolution, 13-band multispectral, updated daily, past-14-months window. No key or account required. Streamed live at view time; wired in November 23, 2026.

Sentinel-2 — color infrared

The same live Sentinel-2 scenes as the layer above, in false colour: the near-infrared band is shown as red, so living vegetation glows red and pink, clear water reads near-black, and the land/water line is unmistakable. OFF by default.

Interpretation method. Identical to the natural-colour layer — per-tile exportImage requests in Web Mercator, no mosaic rule or time filter sent — except the rendering is the service’s “Color Infrared with DRA” raster function (near-infrared, red and green bands shown as red, green and blue). The date shown under the toggle is the same newest-scene acquisition date as the natural-colour layer: the renderings differ, the scenes do not. Caveat: red intensity tracks near-infrared reflectance, not plant health by itself; snow, ice and cloud are bright in this rendering too.

Source: Esri, Sentinel-2 image service — sentinel.arcgis.com/arcgis/rest/services/Sentinel2/ImageServer, raster function “Color Infrared with DRA” (verified live over Leech Lake November 23, 2026). 10 m, updated daily, past-14-months window. Streamed live at view time; wired in November 23, 2026.

Sentinel-2 — NDVI (vegetation)

The same live Sentinel-2 scenes reduced to a vegetation index: NDVI, the normalised difference of near-infrared and red reflectance, drawn on the service’s colour ramp — bare ground and water at the low (red/brown) end, dense green vegetation at the high (green) end. OFF by default.

Interpretation method. Identical streaming and mosaic rules to the natural-colour layer; the rendering is the service’s “NDVI Colormap” raster function, which computes the index from the scene’s bands and colours it on the service’s fixed ramp — the colours are the service’s classification of the index, not a stretch chosen by this viewer. The date under the toggle is the newest scene’s acquisition date, as above. Caveat: NDVI saturates over dense canopy and reads low over water, wet soil and snow; a low value is not by itself a finding about a place.

Source: Esri, Sentinel-2 image service — sentinel.arcgis.com/arcgis/rest/services/Sentinel2/ImageServer, raster function “NDVI Colormap” (verified live over Leech Lake November 23, 2026). 10 m, updated daily, past-14-months window. Streamed live at view time; wired in November 23, 2026.

Sentinel-2 — NDWI (water)

The same live Sentinel-2 scenes reduced to a water index: NDWI, in which open water stands out bright against land — a quick way to read shorelines, flooded ground and which wetlands are holding water in the newest scene. OFF by default.

Interpretation method. Identical streaming and mosaic rules to the natural-colour layer; the rendering is the service’s “NDWI Raw” raster function, which computes the index from the scene’s green and near-infrared bands. The date under the toggle is the newest scene’s acquisition date, as above. Caveat: the index responds to surface water, not depth or permanence; wet soil, ice and cloud shadow can mislead, and a bright pixel is a prompt to look, not a measurement.

Source: Esri, Sentinel-2 image service — sentinel.arcgis.com/arcgis/rest/services/Sentinel2/ImageServer, raster function “NDWI Raw” (verified live over Leech Lake November 23, 2026). 10 m, updated daily, past-14-months window. Streamed live at view time; wired in November 23, 2026.

Sentinel-2 — bathymetric (shallow water)

The same live Sentinel-2 scenes through the service’s bathymetric rendering: a band ratio tuned to penetrate shallow water, so Leech Lake’s shoals, sand bars and shallow bays separate by depth in a way the natural-colour view hides. OFF by default.

Interpretation method. Identical streaming and mosaic rules to the natural-colour layer; the rendering is the service’s “Bathymetric with DRA” raster function. It is a relative rendering, not a depth measurement: brightness differences suggest shallower and deeper water but carry no depth values, and this viewer attaches none. It is a water rendering — land reads washed-out under it. The date under the toggle is the newest scene’s acquisition date, as above. For surveyed depths, use the Bathymetry layers (DNR surveys) instead. Caveat: water clarity, bottom type (sand vs. weed vs. mud) and ice all change the picture independently of depth.

Source: Esri, Sentinel-2 image service — sentinel.arcgis.com/arcgis/rest/services/Sentinel2/ImageServer, raster function “Bathymetric with DRA” (verified live over Leech Lake November 23, 2026). 10 m, updated daily, past-14-months window. Streamed live at view time; wired in November 23, 2026.

Landsat 8/9 — latest imagery

Live satellite imagery from the Landsat program: 30 m natural-colour scenes streamed from Esri’s Landsat image service (USGS/NASA Landsat 8 and 9, with the archive reaching back through earlier Landsats to 1984). Coarser than Sentinel-2’s 10 m, but the archive reaches back decades past Sentinel-2’s 14-month window. OFF by default.

Interpretation method. Each map tile is an exportImage request against the service in Web Mercator (EPSG:3857), rendered with the service’s “Natural Color with DRA” raster function. No mosaic rule and no time filter are sent, so the service’s own mosaic default decides which scene appears: its most recent acquisition. While the layer is on, the panel shows the newest scene over the current view — its acquisition date, the satellite that took it and the scene’s stored cloud-cover percentage — read live from the service’s catalog (its AcquisitionDate, SensorName and CloudCover fields); the mosaic may blend neighbouring scenes, so the date can vary across the view. The route was verified live over Leech Lake on November 23, 2026: the service returned real tiles, and the newest scene over the lake was a Landsat 9 pass of November 13, 2026 with 0.05% cloud cover. Caveat: “latest” means newest, not clearest — in a cloudy stretch the newest scene may be cloudy or weeks old; the cloud-cover figure under the toggle says which.

Source: Esri, Landsat image service (Landsat on AWS via Esri; imagery © USGS and NASA) — landsat.arcgis.com/arcgis/rest/services/Landsat/MS/ImageServer. 30 m resolution, time-enabled, archive to 1984; Landsat 8 (2013–) and Landsat 9 (2021–) revisit a place every 16 days each, 8 days combined. No key or account required. Streamed live at view time; wired in November 23, 2026.

NASA daily true-color (MODIS/VIIRS)

NASA’s daily true-colour picture of the Earth: the MODIS Terra corrected-reflectance layer served by NASA GIBS (the same GIBS service also carries the VIIRS daily layers). At 250 m it is far coarser than Sentinel-2 or Landsat — Leech Lake is a handful of pixels across — but it is a fresh image every day, which makes it the layer for watching snow arrive, ice form and ice go out. OFF by default.

Interpretation method. Tiles are NASA GIBS WMTS tiles (EPSG:3857 “best” endpoint, GoogleMapsCompatible_Level9 matrix set, native to zoom 9; the viewer scales them at closer zooms). The tile URL carries a date, so the viewer first finds the freshest day with imagery: GIBS posts each day’s pass some hours after it is taken and serves a solid-black tile for a day not yet posted, so at first switch-on the viewer probes the tile over Leech Lake for today, then yesterday, and so on, reading the returned image’s pixels (GIBS tiles are CORS-open) — the first day that is not solid black is used, and that date is shown under the toggle. On November 23, 2026 that probe found November 22: the current day’s tile was still black at mid-morning in Minnesota. Caveat: 250 m and daily means weather, not detail — cloud is in most pictures, and nothing smaller than a few hundred metres across can be identified.

Source: NASA Global Imagery Browse Services (GIBS), NASA EOSDIS — gibs.earthdata.nasa.gov/wmts/epsg3857/best, layer MODIS_Terra_CorrectedReflectance_TrueColor (capabilities and tiles verified live November 23, 2026). 250 m, daily, archive to February 2000. No key or account required. Streamed live at view time; wired in November 23, 2026.

Watershed

Watershed — whole basin

The whole Leech Lake River basin: the land whose streams eventually reach Leech Lake and the Leech Lake River below it. One polygon, 857,968 acres (1,341 sq mi). This is the boundary the map opens with.

Interpretation method. The polygon is the Minnesota DNR Major watershed 8, “Leech Lake River,” taken whole from the DNR watersheds service (layer 6, Level 04 HUC08 Majors), query major_name LIKE ‘%Leech%’. No geometry was altered. The federal Watershed Boundary Dataset HUC8 07010102 (“Leech Lake,” 3,473.87 km²) is the cross-reference: the DNR and USGS boundaries agree, because Minnesota’s WBD was derived from DNR catchments and the two were re-aligned in the October 2023 suite update. The 75 DNR minor watersheds inside Major 8 were not used — the largest minor, 8022 “Leech L” (199,606 ac), is a different, smaller delineation than the direct-to-lake subwatershed below and must not be substituted for it.

Source: Minnesota DNR, watersheds (2023 suite) — geos_dnr_watersheds FeatureServer, layer 6. Federal cross-reference: USGS Watershed Boundary Dataset — wbd MapServer, HUC8 07010102. Pulled November 20, 2026. 1 feature.

Watershed — land draining directly to the lake

The subwatershed whose streams run straight into Leech Lake: WBD HUC10 0701010205, “Leech Lake,” 1,346.96 km² (332,840 acres). One polygon.

Interpretation method. The polygon is the WBD HUC10 0701010205 feature taken whole from the WBD MapServer (layer 5), query HUC10=‘0701010205’. No geometry was altered. It is the direct-drainage subwatershed: the tributary HUC10s 0201 (Steamboat River), 0202 (Kabekona River), 0203 (Woman Lake), 0204 (Boy River) and 0206 (Leech Lake River) are separate polygons and are not included. The six HUC10 areas sum exactly to the HUC8 total. A finer DNR Level 09 autocatchment for the lake exists only as a download of pre-2023 vintage and was not used.

Source: USGS Watershed Boundary Dataset — wbd MapServer, layer 5 (HUC10). Pulled November 20, 2026. 1 feature.

Subwatersheds (HUC10)

The five tributary subwatersheds of the basin: Steamboat River (0701010201, 85,857 ac), Kabekona River (0701010202, 77,262 ac), Woman Lake (0701010203, 104,372 ac), Boy River (0701010204, 148,808 ac) and Leech Lake River (0701010206, 109,273 ac). Together with the direct-to-lake subwatershed above (0205), the six HUC10 areas sum exactly to the HUC8 total.

Interpretation method. The five polygons are the WBD HUC10 features taken whole from the WBD MapServer (layer 5), query HUC10 IN (‘0701010201’,‘0701010202’,‘0701010203’,‘0701010204’,‘0701010206’). No geometry was altered and no clip was applied — HUC10s are within the HUC8 by definition. HUC10 0701010205 is deliberately NOT in this file: it is already mapped as the “land draining directly to the lake” layer above, and including it twice would double-draw it.

Source: USGS Watershed Boundary Dataset — wbd MapServer, layer 5 (HUC10). Pulled November 21, 2026. 5 features.

Lakes

Lakes

Lakes and ponds in the watershed: 2,545 polygons. 501 carry a basin name in the source; of those, 344 are a real name and 157 are recorded as “Unnamed.” Leech Lake is drawn as one whole-lake outline.

Interpretation method. Source is the DNR Hydrography service, layer 1 (“Dnr Hydro Features All”), filtered to wb_class=‘Lake or Pond’ and clipped to the WBD HUC8 07010102 polygon (spatial intersect). The in-watershed clip returned 2,549 features; four were then dropped — the Leech Lake bay sub-polygons “Leech (Main Basin)” DOW 11020301, “Leech (Kabekona Bay)” 11020302, “Leech (Ah-Gwah-Ching)” 11020303 and “Leech (Shingobee Bay)” 11020304 — because they overlap the whole-lake record and must not be summed with it. The kept Leech Lake record is the whole-lake DOW 11020300: 103,443.8 acres, 194.9 shore miles, Cass County.

Which Leech Lake outline. The DNR publishes a second, larger outline in its Large Lakes service: 110,311.4 acres, 232.7 shore miles — about 7% larger, a different delineation vintage and method (the commonly cited ~111,500 acres matches that outline more closely). This viewer uses the Hydrography whole-lake outline (103,443.8 acres, DOW 11020300), because it is the record inside the lake layer itself, and it says so wherever the lake’s area is shown. The Large Lakes outline was NOT used.

Source: Minnesota DNR, Hydrography — water_dnr_hydrography FeatureServer, layer 1. Lake pages: DNR LakeFinder, keyed by DOW number (Leech Lake: showreport.html?downum=11020300). Pulled November 20, 2026. 2,545 features. Cross-check: USGS NHD Waterbody, Large Scale (layer 12) — 4,862 features in the same bounding box, 559 named, corroborating the DNR count in magnitude.

Infested waters

Waters the DNR lists as infested with aquatic invasive species: 12 rows in the watershed (one row per waterbody and species). Three are for Leech Lake itself — Eurasian watermilfoil (designated 2005), zebra mussel (2016) and starry stonewort (2021). The other rows: Town Line (milfoil), Benedict, Garfield, Steamboat, Ten Mile and Woman (zebra mussel), Girl (milfoil and zebra mussel), and the Leech Lake River between Mud Lake and the Mississippi River (faucet snail).

Interpretation method. Source is the DNR listed-infested-waters service (layer 0, polygons), clipped to the WBD HUC8 07010102 polygon. Rows are drawn exactly as the service returns them — one polygon per waterbody-and-species row, with the service’s species, designation and confirmation dates. No species claim was added or changed. Caveat: the DNR’s published list is the authoritative record, not this GIS layer — the layer’s own metadata was last updated February 24, 2017 and warns it was about 98% complete as of January 2017, with some rivers and streams missing. Cross-check any per-lake species claim against the DNR’s current spreadsheet (last updated October 21, 2026) before publishing it. Note: the pull manifest’s caveat line said “Leech Lake rows: 0”; the file itself holds three Leech rows (milfoil, zebra mussel, starry stonewort) — the file is authoritative.

Source: Minnesota DNR, listed infested waters — env_listed_infested_waters FeatureServer, layer 0. Official record: DNR infested waters list (spreadsheet, updated October 21, 2026). Pulled November 20, 2026. 12 features.

Bathymetry — depth contours

DNR lake bathymetry depth contours for the surveyed lakes in the watershed: 1,590 contour polylines across 85 lakes and basins, depths 0–200 ft below the surface. The deepest contour in the watershed is 200 ft. Contours are drawn every 5 ft; in deeper water (below about 110 ft in some lakes) the spacing widens to 10 ft — the depths present run 0, 5, 10 … 140 ft in 5-ft steps, then 150, 160, 170, 180 and 200 ft. Clicking a contour shows its depth. The layer is OFF by default: the file is about 9.9 MB, so it loads only when switched on.

Interpretation method. Source is the DNR lake bathymetry service, layer 0 (contours), clipped to the WBD HUC8 07010102 polygon (spatial intersect). Contours are drawn exactly as returned, with the service’s own fields: lake name, DNR lake number (dowlknum), depth (negative feet below the surface, 0 at the shoreline) and abs_depth (the positive magnitude) — the popups show abs_depth, so a contour reads “40 ft deep” rather than “−40 ft.” No contours were merged, smoothed or reclassified. Leech Lake’s contours are split by basin in the source — Main Basin (DOW 11020301) and Kabekona Bay (11020302) — not the whole-lake DOW 11020300 the Lakes layer uses. Note the service is MapServer-only: the FeatureServer endpoint on the same service errors (HTTP 500), so the pull used the MapServer query endpoint.

Source: Minnesota DNR, lake bathymetry — water_lake_bathymetry MapServer, layer 0. Pulled November 21, 2026. 1,590 features.

Bathymetry — surveyed lakes

The outlines of the lakes the DNR holds bathymetry surveys for: 144 outline polygons covering 85 distinct lakes and basins. Islands are drawn as separate features (flagged island=‘Y’) sharing their lake’s DNR number, which is why there are more outlines than lakes. Clicking an outline shows the lake name and, where the DNR publishes it, the survey information: when the survey’s fieldwork ended, the survey map number, and the data source. The layer is OFF by default.

Interpretation method. Source is the DNR lake bathymetry service, layer 1 (outlines), clipped to the WBD HUC8 07010102 polygon (spatial intersect). The survey metadata lives in a separate table in the same service (layer 2) and was joined onto the outlines by DNR lake number (dowlknum) at pull time: 76 of the 144 outlines carry survey metadata; every metadata record found a matching outline. Where an outline has no metadata, its popup simply omits the survey rows — no survey date or map number was inferred. Leech Lake’s survey: map B0050, fieldwork ended July 18, 1957, data source “ORIG MAP” (the original survey map).

Source: Minnesota DNR, lake bathymetry — water_lake_bathymetry MapServer, layers 1 (outlines) and 2 (survey metadata). Pulled November 21, 2026. 144 features.

Impaired lakes (MPCA 2024)

Lakes on the MPCA’s 2024 final impaired waters list (the 303(d) list) in the watershed: 28 lakes — 20 in Category 4A and 8 in Category 5. Six are listed among the impaired lakes partially within a reservation. Leech Lake’s basins are on the list for mercury in fish tissue (Hg-F), listed in 1998: the Main Basin (11-0203-01, Category 4A) sits in the reservation layer of the source service, which is why it was missed by pulls of the main impaired-lakes layer alone. Hart Lake is the one nutrient impairment, listed in 2016 (Category 5).

Interpretation method. Two layers of the MPCA impaired waters 2024 service were merged into one file: layer 13 (impaired lakes) and layer 12 (impaired lakes partially within reservations); the merged file’s in_reservation field (‘Y’ on 6 lakes) marks the layer-12 features, and each impaired-lake popup shows that flag. Both layers were clipped to the WBD HUC8 07010102 polygon. Lakes are coloured by the service’s Integrated Report category field (cat): 4A orange (impaired, TMDL approved), 5 red (impaired, TMDL required). imp_param is the authoritative list of impairment parameters and is shown verbatim in the popups. Listing years, not flags: the per-pollutant columns (hg_f, nutrients, e_coli and the rest) carry the year the impairment was listed, not a yes/no flag — so Leech’s Hg-F reads 1998 and Hart’s nutrients read 2016, and the popups present them as listing years. Caveat: this is the 2024 final list, not the 2026 draft.

Source: Minnesota Pollution Control Agency, Impaired Waters 2024 — env_impaired_water_2024 FeatureServer, layers 13 and 12. Pulled November 21, 2026. 28 features.

Assessed lakes (MPCA 2024)

Every lake the MPCA assessed in the watershed, impaired or not: 85 lakes. Each lake carries a per-use assessment — aquatic recreation (AQR), aquatic life (AQL), aquatic consumption (AQC), wild rice (WR) and drinking water (DW) — with a support finding (FS fully supporting, NS not supporting, IF insufficient information), a per-use Integrated Report category and a last assessment year. In this data, 74 of the 85 lakes carry AQR category 2; the layer makes no summary headline claim beyond the per-lake fields themselves.

Interpretation method. Source is the MPCA assessed waters 2024 service, layer 5, clipped to the WBD HUC8 07010102 polygon, drawn exactly as returned. Because an assessed lake has several per-use categories rather than one, the fill colour is the worst (most impaired) of the per-use categories present (5 > 4A > 4D/4C > 3 > 2), using the same category colours as the impaired layers; the popup shows every assessed use separately with its own support, category and last assessment year, so the colour can always be traced back to the field that produced it.

Source: Minnesota Pollution Control Agency, Assessed Waters 2024 — env_assessed_water_2024 FeatureServer, layer 5. Pulled November 21, 2026. 85 features.

Water-quality monitoring stations

MPCA surface-water monitoring stations in the watershed sampled in 2015 or later: 217 points — 164 lake, 24 stream, 26 discharge and 3 biological stations.

Interpretation method — the filter rule. The unfiltered in-watershed count is 622 stations, which is too many to plot raw. The filter applied at pull time, all station types: latest_sampling_year >= 2015. That leaves 217 stations. This is a documented judgment call (the source audit’s open question 4); Paul may override it — a different year, a type restriction, or named waters only would change the count, and the rule must be re-stated if it changes. No other filtering was applied: every station type is shown, coloured by type in the popups and legend.

Clip note. The MPCA service rejects the full 4,743-vertex HUC8 polygon as an invalid geometry parameter, so the clip used the HUC8 polygon simplified to 100 m (734 vertices). Tolerance sensitivity on the unfiltered count: 100 m gives 622, 500 m gives 623, 1,000 m gives 622 — at most one boundary station differs, so the simplification does not materially change the layer. The bounding-box count (726) was re-verified live and is larger only because the box includes ground outside the watershed.

Source: Minnesota Pollution Control Agency, EDA surface water stations — env_eda_surfacewater_stations FeatureServer, layer 0. Per-station reports: MPCA EDA, linked from each popup (url_text). Pulled November 20, 2026. 217 features (unfiltered in-watershed: 622). DNR LakeFinder is a link-out reference only, not a layer: it is a per-lake lookup keyed by DOW number. USGS NWIS sites are layers of their own — see the surface-gauge and groundwater-well sections below.

MPCA sites (What’s in My Neighborhood)

MPCA What’s in My Neighborhood (WIMN) sites in the watershed: 813 points — Cass 645, Hubbard 158, Beltrami 10. By program: Stormwater 298, Hazardous Waste 142, Tanks 130, Investigation and Cleanup 70, SSTS 67, Multiple Programs 58, Feedlots 21, Water Quality 13, Solid Waste 13, Air Quality 1. The largest single activity is Construction Stormwater (283 sites), followed by Hazardous Waste (117) and Underground Tanks (87); 38 sites are Petroleum Remediation leak sites. Each popup shows the site’s name, activity, program, county and address, and links back to that site’s own MPCA page.

Interpretation method. Source is the MPCA What’s in My Neighborhood service (layer 0), queried by the service’s own huc8 attribute (huc8 = ‘07010102’) — not a polygon clip. The cross-check query watershed_name LIKE ‘%Leech%’ returns the same 812 sites. Sites are drawn exactly as returned, with the service’s own fields: name, address, county, activity, program and MPCA ID. For sites with more than one activity or program, the popups show the service’s full activity and program lists rather than the generic “Multiple Activities” / “Multiple Programs” labels. One site inside the watershed polygon carries NULL huc8 / watershed_name in the service, so the attribute query misses it; it was appended on November 21, 2026 after a live fetch of its full record plus the spatial-clip check — “Anderson Brothers Construction Co Base 6” (site_id 84, 5 miles S of Hackensack, Cass County, −94.4716, 46.9465; Multiple Activities / Multiple Programs; MPCA site page). A spatial clip returns 813 against 812 by attribute; no attribute-query site falls outside the polygon. That one site’s coordinates are a zip-code centroid in the service record, so its point is approximate.

What this database is — and is not. This is a sites database, not a live spill feed: it lists contaminated properties (still being investigated and already cleaned up), petroleum tank leak sites, open and closed landfills, hazardous-waste sites, permitted and registered facilities, facilities the MPCA has penalized, and emergency-management activities — which is where spill responses appear. Individual spill reports come in through the Minnesota Duty Officer and surface here as emergency-management activities. Agricultural-chemical spills are a separate database run by the Minnesota Department of Agriculture, not the MPCA, and are not in this layer. Listing in the database does not imply a current threat — follow the popup’s link to the MPCA page for a site’s current status.

Source: Minnesota Pollution Control Agency, What’s in My Neighborhood — env_my_neighborhood FeatureServer, layer 0. Dataset listing: MPCA, What’s in My Neighborhood — about page (the Commons’ old env-my-neighborhood listing page no longer resolves — it redirects to the Commons homepage — so the link goes to the MPCA application’s about page, the same link the layer’s info button uses). Per-site pages: MPCA WIMN, linked from each popup (site_url). Pulled November 21, 2026. 813 features.

Impaired streams (MPCA 2024)

Stream reaches on the MPCA’s 2024 final impaired waters list (303(d)) in the watershed: 3 reaches — the Kabekona River (Category 5, E. coli, listed 2016), Spring Creek (Category 4D, invertebrate bioassessment) and one unnamed creek (Category 4D, fish and invertebrate bioassessment).

Census

The census layers were pulled on November 21, 2026: boundaries from the Census Bureau’s TIGER/Line 2024 cartographic boundary files, values from the American Community Survey (ACS) 2024 5-year estimates and the 2020 Census PL 94-171 full counts, fetched through the Census API and joined to the boundaries by GEOID. All census layers are OFF by default.

The governing rule — no clipping, no allocation. Census units do not follow the watershed boundary, and cutting them would fabricate precision: a block group split by the boundary cannot be honestly divided without modelling this map does not have. Census units therefore render whole — the full block-group and tract sets for Beltrami, Cass and Hubbard counties — and the watershed polygon mutes what lies outside it (the basin layer’s dimming is drawn over the census fills), rather than hiding or cutting it. Every number in a census popup belongs to a whole census unit, never to an invented watershed fraction.

Margins of error. Every ACS estimate is stored with its margin of error (the API fields ending in M) and every popup shows it. At block-group level the margins are large — often ±30–50% of the estimate — and they are part of the figure, not fine print. The derived shares (seasonal share, vacancy rate, owner share, under-18 and 65+ shares) are computed from the stored estimates; the under-18 and 65+ counts carry root-sum-of-squares MOEs from their summed age bands, and the share margins shown in the popups use the Census Bureau’s derived-proportion approximation. The Census API’s not-applicable sentinel (−666666666) is stored as null and simply not shown. The 2020 PL figures are full counts from the April 1, 2020 enumeration — a usual-residence count, so the summer population is invisible in them — and carry no margin of error.

Colouring — the variable switcher. A “Colour the map by” dropdown at the head of the map’s Census group recolours the block groups, the tracts and the reservation together, and the legend scale follows the chosen variable. The default is the seasonal share of housing. The choices — every one already stored in the two census data files, no new pull — are: median household income (B19013_001E, MOE B19013_001M); seasonal share of housing (seasonal_share); median age (B01002_001E); population (B01001_001E); population density (the stored population divided by the unit’s land area, ALAND — people per square mile; the reservation record carries no land-area field, so it has no density and draws in the no-data grey on that variable); vacancy rate (vacancy_rate); owner-occupied share (owner_share); share under 18 (share_under18) and share 65 and over (share_65plus); average household size (B25010_001E); median year built (B25035_001E); and total housing units (B25001_001E). Class breaks are fixed round numbers spanning the combined tract and block-group ranges, so the two geographies read on one comparable scale. Popups are unchanged by the switcher: every popup still shows all estimates with their margins of error, and the reservation record keeps its own popup values.

Extension variables (pulled November 21, 2026). A second pull the same day added five more switcher variables, bringing the stored set to 106 ACS variables: Native American share (share_native, from B02001 race), Hispanic or Latino share (share_hispanic, from B03003), poverty rate (poverty_rate, from B17001), and educational attainment for the population 25 and over from B15003 — bachelor’s degree or higher (share_ba_plus) and high school diploma or higher (share_hs_plus). Each share is stored with its derived margin of error (the _moe twin field) and every popup shows the new estimates with those margins, alongside the underlying counts for the two education measures. Ranges across the watershed’s units: Native American share runs to 96% in the highest block group; Hispanic share stays under 10% everywhere; bachelor’s-or-higher runs 4–78% in block groups and 7–49% in tracts; high-school-or-higher runs 73–100%.

Poverty is tract-level only. The Census Bureau does not publish B17001 at block-group level — verified in this pull: the poverty rate is null in all 84 block groups and populated in all 32 tracts (5.2%–32.5%). The poverty switcher option therefore colours the tracts; block groups draw in the no-data grey, each block-group popup states plainly that poverty is not published at block-group level, and no block-group poverty value is estimated or invented anywhere in this map. The reservation, tabulated as its own area, does carry a poverty rate: 20.9% (±2.5 pts).

Income — read the tracts. Block-group income is the least reliable figure on this map: median household income at block-group level carries margins of error of ±30–50% of the estimate, so neighbouring block groups that look far apart on the income colouring may not differ at all once the margin is read. Tracts are the reliable view for income — the same estimate over bigger populations, with correspondingly smaller margins. The switcher says so in the panel whenever income is chosen, and every income figure in every popup carries its margin of error beside it.

The mosaics as first wired carried every sheet’s collar — the margins with the sheet name, scale bar, legend and index boxes — and in the flattened mosaic each sheet’s collar lay across its neighbours’ edges, so the quads read as separate sheets separated by cream (1968–72) or white (1996, 1:100,000) strips. The tiles were reprocessed in place: everything outside the sheets’ neatline grid was made transparent, and the collar bands found inside a quad’s neatline — detected as paper-coloured bands hugging a neatline across the quad’s full span (interior rows and columns score near zero on the same measure) — were made transparent as well. On the 1968–72 and 1:100,000 mosaics a further pass cleared the fixed-depth collar-debris bands (a neighbour’s text blocks, scale bar and index insets lying inside the neatline) where their paper density proved them. The 1996 mosaic was deliberately kept to the conservative crop: there the debris lies interleaved with the sheets’ own pale faces, and the stronger passes cut real map face along with the debris when they were tried — so a few collar remnants survive along some 1996 quad edges rather than lose the face under them. What a neighbour’s collar had already covered in the flattened mosaic cannot be recovered, so a narrow see-through gap remains along some quad edges where the collar lay; everywhere else the quads now meet at their neatlines.
